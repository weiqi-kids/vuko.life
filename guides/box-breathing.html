Box Breathing (4-4-4-4): Steps and When to Use It

By the Vuko team · Updated August 3, 2026

Box breathing is a paced-breathing method with four equal phases: inhale for 4 seconds, hold for 4, exhale for 4, and hold again for 4, like tracing the sides of a square. Practiced for one to five minutes, it steadies the nervous system and sharpens focus — which is why Navy SEALs, athletes, and clinicians teach it for acute stress.

What is box breathing?

Box breathing — also called square breathing or four-square breathing — is one of the simplest breath-control patterns there is. Each of the four phases lasts the same count, which is what makes it feel like a "box": four sides of equal length. The technique became widely known through the U.S. Navy SEALs, where former commander Mark Divine popularized it as a tool for staying composed under pressure, but the underlying pattern (sama vritti, "equal breath") comes from yoga and has been taught for far longer.

Unlike sedating patterns built around a long exhale, box breathing is balanced. That balance is its personality: it calms you down without switching you off, which makes it the go-to choice when you need to stay sharp — before a presentation, in a difficult meeting, between points in a match.

Step-by-step instructions

  1. Sit upright with both feet on the floor, shoulders relaxed. Exhale fully through your mouth to start empty. Let the breath come from the diaphragm rather than the upper chest.
  2. Inhale for 4. Breathe in slowly through your nose for a count of 4, letting your belly — not your shoulders — expand.
  3. Hold for 4. Keep your lungs full for a count of 4. Stay soft; no clenching your throat.
  4. Exhale for 4. Release the breath steadily through your mouth or nose for a count of 4.
  5. Hold for 4. Pause with lungs empty for a count of 4, then begin the next inhale.
  6. Repeat for 1–5 minutes — roughly 4 to 15 rounds. Finish whenever your breathing feels settled.

Many people find it helps to visualize drawing a square, one side per phase, or to trace a square on their leg with a fingertip. The visualization is not decoration — giving your attention a concrete track is a large part of why the technique quiets mental chatter.

Why it works

Slow, paced breathing at around four to six breaths per minute stimulates the vagus nerve and the baroreflex, the reflex loop researchers credit for turning that vagal signal into a slower heart rate, producing the breath-linked oscillation called respiratory sinus arrhythmia, increasing heart-rate variability, and shifting the autonomic nervous system toward its parasympathetic, "rest and digest" branch — measurably lowering the physiological signature of stress. Box breathing at 4-4-4-4 lands close to that range (just under four breaths per minute). The two holds do additional work: the hold with lungs empty briefly lets carbon dioxide build up, nudging the chemoreceptors that normally trigger the urge to breathe; both holds also interrupt the shallow, rapid chest-breathing loop that anxiety produces, and force enough attention onto counting that rumination loses its grip. The same slow pace is what the eight-week trial behind this page's focus claims measured directly, leaving the practice group with lower cortisol than untrained controls. The result most people report is not sleepiness but a kind of reset — lower arousal, clearer head.

When to use it (and when not to)

  • Before a stressful event: presentations, interviews, exams, difficult conversations. Two minutes beforehand is often enough.
  • During acute stress: after an alarming email, in traffic, mid-conflict — anywhere you can take four slow breaths.
  • To regain focus: as a reset between tasks or before deep work.
  • As daily practice: five minutes a day builds the reflex so it is available under real pressure.

When not to: box breathing is less ideal as a pure sleep-onset tool, because its symmetry keeps you composed rather than drowsy. At bedtime, a long-exhale pattern such as 4-7-8 breathing or the techniques in our falling-asleep guide usually works better. And as with any breath-holding, do not practice while driving or in water.

Variations

  • 3-3-3-3: a gentler box if 4-second holds feel strained — common for beginners.
  • 5-5-5-5 or 6-6-6-6: a slower box as your comfort grows; stays effective as long as it feels smooth, not forced.
  • 4-4-4 (triangle): drop the empty hold if that phase makes you air-hungry.
  • With sound pacing: some people prefer following audio instead of counting. Key takeaway: box breathing is a four-sided pattern — inhale 4, hold 4, exhale 4, hold 4 — that lowers stress while keeping you alert. Use it for one to five minutes before or during pressure moments; shorten the counts if you feel strained, and prefer a long-exhale technique when your goal is sleep rather than composure.

Frequently asked questions

How long should I do box breathing?
One to five minutes is the usual range — about 4 to 15 rounds. For acute stress, even three or four rounds noticeably steadies most people. As a daily practice, five minutes once or twice a day is plenty.
Is box breathing better than 4-7-8 breathing?
They serve different moments. Box breathing's equal phases keep you calm and alert, making it better for daytime stress and focus. 4-7-8's extra-long exhale is more sedating, making it better before sleep. Many people use both.
Why do Navy SEALs use box breathing?
Because it is a fast, equipment-free way to bring heart rate and attention under control in high-stress situations. The counting structure gives the mind a concrete task, and the paced rhythm blunts the fight-or-flight response without causing drowsiness.
Can box breathing make you dizzy?
The breath holds can cause mild light-headedness at first, especially if you inhale too deeply. Breathe at a normal depth, shorten the counts to 3-3-3-3 if needed, practice seated, and skip the holds if you are pregnant or have a cardiovascular or respiratory condition until you have medical advice.

References

Why slow, paced breathing calms you

  • Zaccaro A, Piarulli A, Laurino M, et al. How breath-control can change your life: a systematic review on psycho-physiological correlates of slow breathing. Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, 2018. Screened 2,461 papers and kept 15 that met its criteria. Breathing under 10 breaths per minute was consistently linked to higher heart-rate variability and lower self-reported anxiety and anger.
  • Lehrer PM, Gevirtz R. Heart rate variability biofeedback: how and why does it work? Frontiers in Psychology, 2014. Explains why paced breathing at around six or seven breaths a minute produces an outsized heart-rate-variability response, and why biofeedback training targets each person's own resonance rate rather than one fixed number.
  • Gerritsen RJS, Band GPH. Breath of life: the respiratory vagal stimulation model of contemplative activity. Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, 2018. A theoretical model of how breath-hold length changes vagus-nerve stimulation. Box breathing's two holds are exactly the variable this model addresses, though it is a framework paper rather than a measured trial.
  • Yasuma F, Hayano J. Respiratory sinus arrhythmia: why does the heartbeat synchronize with respiratory rhythm? Chest, 2004. Explains the heart-rate-slowing mechanism that a paced exhale draws on.
  • Trembach N, Zabolotskikh I. Evaluation of breath-holding test in assessment of peripheral chemoreflex sensitivity. Open Respiratory Medicine Journal, 2017. Uses breath-hold duration to measure how sensitive the body's chemoreceptors are to rising carbon dioxide — the same detection mechanism box breathing's empty-lung hold engages.

Attention and focus

  • Ma X, Yue ZQ, Gong ZQ, et al. The effect of diaphragmatic breathing on attention, negative affect and stress in healthy adults. Frontiers in Psychology, 2017. A 40-person randomized trial. Eight weeks of diaphragmatic breathing training at an average of 4 breaths per minute produced significantly lower cortisol, less negative affect, and better sustained attention than a control group. This is the study behind this guide's focus claims, and it tested belly-breathing practice specifically rather than box breathing itself.
